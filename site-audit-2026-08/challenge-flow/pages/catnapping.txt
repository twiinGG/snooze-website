# Catnapping: why your baby wakes after 20 minutes and what to do first

A catnap is a single sleep cycle that ends when your baby surfaces into light sleep and does not link into the next one. Short naps are completely normal in the early months and often need no fixing. A stuck 20 to 30 minute pattern in older babies is worth working on because it can chip away at night sleep.

Written by Sally Woods, The Sleep Concierge &middot

## What's going on

A catnap is one sleep cycle. Baby drifts down into deep sleep, comes back up to the surface at the light-sleep join between cycles and then wakes fully instead of sliding into a second cycle. Short naps are not automatically a problem. In young babies they are simply how sleep is built, and a baby who catnaps but feeds well, grows well and is content between sleeps is doing fine. The pattern worth working on is a baby past the newborn stage who keeps cutting naps short, wakes cranky rather than rested and starts losing ground at night.

On the consult floor, a common trap is stretching wake windows to force a longer nap. It feels logical that a more tired baby should sleep longer, but it usually backfires and feeds an over-tired loop where naps get shorter rather than longer. More often the fix runs the other way. Easing the pressure before a nap tends to do more for the second sleep cycle than piling more onto it.

## Is this normal?

### Usually normal

- Newborn catnaps through most of the day. Focus on safe sleep and feeding, not nap length.

- Short naps during a known regression or nap transition. Hold your rhythm steady and wait it out.

### Worth working on

- Stuck 20 to 30 minute naps in an older baby who wakes cranky. Log naps and check total daytime sleep.

- Catnapping alongside an early morning wake. Review nap timing across the whole day.

## First steps you can take today

- Log naps for a week. Write down when each nap started, how long it ran and how your baby woke. Patterns show up in the log that daily memory misses, and you will often see the short naps clustering around the same time of day.

- Add up total daytime sleep. A baby who takes several short naps may be getting plenty of day sleep overall. Knowing the daily total tells you whether you are looking at a real shortfall or just a short-but-frequent napper.

- Check the tiredness fit. Note whether longer awake stretches make naps worse rather than better. If they do, an over-tired loop is the more likely driver than a baby who needs even more awake time.

- Rule out the obvious. Teething, illness, hunger and a nappy that needs changing all cut naps short. Clear these before you treat a run of short naps as a pattern to work on.

## Catnapping FAQs

Quick answers parents ask when every nap ends at the 20 to 30 minute mark.

What is catnapping in babies?

Catnapping is when a nap ends after a single sleep cycle, often around 20 to 40 minutes,
because your baby surfaces into light sleep and does not link into the next cycle.
Short naps are common in young babies. A stuck short-nap pattern in an older baby who
wakes cranky is the pattern worth working on.

Is a 20 minute nap normal?

Yes for many newborns and young babies, especially if they feed well, grow well and are
content between sleeps. It is more of a concern when short naps drag on past the early
months, leave your baby overtired, and start chipping away at night sleep.

Should I stretch wake windows to fix catnapping?

Usually not as the first move. Making a baby more tired to force a longer nap often
backfires and feeds an over-tired loop. Easing pressure before the nap and checking the
whole day's timing tends to help more than piling on awake time.

Can catnapping cause early rising or night wakes?

It can. When daytime sleep is fragmented, overnight sleep often fragments too. If short
naps travel with early mornings, review nap timing across the whole day rather than
treating the morning wake in isolation. See our
early rising guide as well.

What should I try first for short naps?

Log naps for a week, add up total daytime sleep, check whether longer awake stretches
make naps worse, and rule out teething, illness, hunger or a wet nappy. Those four steps
tell you whether you are looking at a normal young-baby pattern or a stuck loop to work on.

When should I get more help for catnapping?

If your baby is past the newborn stage, short naps are stuck, nights are suffering, and
the first steps are not shifting things, it is reasonable to use a fuller plan and support.
Poor weight gain or feeding trouble is not a nap-length issue, speak to your GP or child
health nurse. For sleep planning support, explore the
Snooze membership.
