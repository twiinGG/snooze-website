# Nap transitions: when to drop a nap and what to do first

A nap transition is when your baby is ready to drop a nap and reshape the day around fewer sleeps. It happens in stages as they grow, from the first drop in the early months through to the last nap in the toddler years. Most transition trouble is timing, not readiness. Track the day before you change anything.

Reviewed by Sally Woods, The Sleep Concierge &middot updated Jul 2026

## What's going on

A nap transition is a genuine shift in how much daytime sleep your baby needs, not a rough patch. Readiness tends to show up as a steady pattern: your baby fights a nap day after day, while still staying happy and rested on the sleep they do get. A false transition looks similar for a few days then passes, from teething, a cold or a developmental leap. Often the difference is time. A true transition holds and keeps building, a rough patch fades.

On the consult floor, a common trap is dropping a nap to solve a bedtime battle. A baby who fights bedtime looks like a baby who needs less sleep, so the nap goes. In truth the day was already a touch under-slept, the nap was holding things together and pulling it makes both the nap and the night worse. The other common pattern is the nap stack, pushing the remaining naps closer together to hold on to the old number of naps: the sleeps get shorter and choppier and the baby ends up more wired than rested.

## Is this normal?

### Usually normal

- Naps run short while the day is resettling. Offer a little more awake time or quiet play before the nap.

- Bedtime naturally wants to move earlier. Hold bedtime steady for a few days then ease it earlier if the tiredness is real.

- Toddler skips the nap but crashes hard at night. Offer quiet rest time in the room instead of forcing sleep.

### Worth working on

- Your baby fights the new nap time. Shift the nap start in small steps rather than one big jump.

- Early rising shows up right after a drop. Look at nap timing and total sleep before anything else.

- Overtiredness spikes mid-transition. Ease back the awake time a touch and protect the next sleep.

## First steps you can take today

- Track the day for about a week. Note wake time, nap starts, nap lengths and bedtime. A pattern on paper tells you far more than the memory of one hard afternoon, and it shows whether the resistance is every day or just a bad run.

- Move in small steps, not big jumps. When it really is time, shift the nap a little at a time rather than pulling it all at once. A gradual move gives the body clock room to catch up and keeps overtiredness from piling on.

- Protect bedtime through the wobble. A dropped nap leaves a gap, so hold bedtime steady and bring it a touch earlier if your baby is genuinely flagging. Guarding the night keeps the whole routine from drifting while the day resettles.

- Watch the total sleep, not the nap count. Add up night sleep plus naps. If the day comes up short once a nap goes, the fix is more rest somewhere, not pushing on with a schedule that no longer adds up.
