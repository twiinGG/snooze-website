# Sleep regressions: what they really are and what to do first

Most of what parents call a regression is really a schedule your baby has outgrown, or a new skill breaking through at night. The 4 month change is the exception: a permanent shift in how your baby sleeps, not a phase to wait out. Check the schedule first.

Reviewed by Sally Woods, The Sleep Concierge &middot updated Jul 2026

## What's going on

A regression is a real, temporary disruption to settled sleep that lines up with a burst of development. The catch is that most of what parents call a regression is not one: it is usually a schedule the baby has outgrown, or a new skill breaking through in the night. The 4 month change is the exception. It is the one true reset: around 4 months your baby's sleep cycles become more mature, with lighter phases between them, and that is permanent, not a phase to wait out.

Three things sit behind most regressions: an outgrown schedule, where wake windows that fit last month leave your baby overtired this month and overtiredness looks exactly like a regression; development, where a new motor skill, a language leap or separation awareness surfaces at night as your baby rehearses it; and the 4 month maturation, a permanent change in sleep architecture rather than a passing driver. On the consult floor, a common trap is a regression that turns out to be a schedule the baby quietly outgrew. That is why the first move is always to audit the schedule before you settle in to wait.

## Is this normal?

### Usually normal

- Waking through the night again around 4 months, after a settled stretch. Treat it as a new baseline and steady the daytime rhythm.

- Suddenly fighting or refusing a nap around 8 to 10 months. Give the skill plenty of practice in awake time and keep offering the nap.

- Night wakings that started when a new motor skill did. Practise the skill during the day so nights feel less novel.

- Screaming or stalling at bedtime around 18 months. Hold the routine and the limit calmly and consistently.

### Worth working on

- An early morning wake that appeared during a regression. Check the schedule before assuming the regression caused it.

### When to call your GP

- Fever, refusing feeds, losing weight or signs of dehydration. This is not a regression. Contact your GP.

## First steps you can take today

- Log three to five days first. Note wake time, naps, feeds, night wakings and bedtime. A short log tells a true regression apart from a schedule that has slipped, which memory alone will miss.

- Audit the schedule before you assume a regression. Overtiredness from a day that no longer fits is a common regression impostor, so check that first. If the wake windows that worked last month feel too short now, the day, not a phase, is the problem.

- Look for the developmental marker. A genuine regression usually pairs with a new skill: rolling, crawling, pulling up, first words or a fresh burst of separation awareness. Once you can spot the skill you can stop bracing and start supporting it.

- Rule out teething and illness. Sore gums and a cold can ride alongside a leap and muddy the picture. Check for fever, feed refusal or obvious pain before you pin everything on development.

## Free help for the regression you are in

Regressions do not all behave the same way, so start with the one in front of you.

### Around 4 months

Sleep cycles mature and the wakes multiply. This is the one regression that is a permanent change rather than a phase, so it is worth meeting it with a plan.

### 18 months and 2 years

These two are behavioural as much as biological. Both turn on the schedule underneath them and the boundary you hold when it is tested.
