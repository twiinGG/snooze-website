Parents struggling with an overtired, unsettled baby who won't sleep despite being exhausted.

---

## Orientation Lesson

Sound familiar?

Here's what's probably happening. Your baby is overtired.

It seems counterintuitive, right? You'd think a really tired baby would just *sleep*. But that's not how infant biology works. When babies stay awake too long, their little bodies release cortisol, the stress hormone. And cortisol is basically like giving your baby a tiny espresso. Suddenly they're wired, fighting sleep, and crying their little eyes out.

The good news? This is fixable.

In this Free Module, I'm going to teach you about wake windows. How long your baby can actually handle being awake at each age, and how to spot the difference between "sleepy" and "hot mess express."

When you get the timing right, everything changes. Settling gets easier. Naps get longer. Nights get calmer.

Now, wake windows are one piece of the puzzle. Your baby's sleep is also shaped by their environment, their feeds, their bedtime routine. If you want to look at the whole picture, my full Newborn Guide has you covered.

But for now? Let's nail these wake windows. You'll be amazed at the difference it makes.

### Lesson Text Body

**Welcome to Your Free Module: Wake Windows By Age**

Hi, I'm Sally, and I'm so glad you found your way here.

If you clicked on this module, you're probably in the thick of it. A baby who seems constantly tired but won't settle. Cranky, crying, arching their back, fighting every sleep like their life depends on it.

Here's what's going on: your baby is likely overtired.

**What is overtiredness?**

When babies stay awake longer than they can handle, their body releases cortisol, the stress hormone. It's your baby's way of staying alert to "survive" the perceived threat of being awake too long. The problem is, cortisol makes it much harder for them to actually fall asleep and *stay* asleep.

An overtired baby might:
- Cry inconsolably
- Arch their back and stiffen
- Seem "wired" or hyperactive
- Refuse to close their eyes even though they're clearly exhausted

The antidote? Getting them to sleep *before* they hit that wall.

**What you'll learn in this module:**

- Age-appropriate wake windows from birth to 3 months
- How to read your baby's "ready for sleep" cues vs "too far gone" cues
- Practical tips to catch that window before cortisol kicks in

**Timing is everything**

Newborns can only handle 45-75 minutes of awake time. That's not very long. By the time you've changed a nappy, done a feed, and had a quick cuddle, the window is closing.

When you start watching the clock *and* your baby's cues, you'll find that settling becomes so much easier. You're no longer fighting biology. You're working with it.

**Sleep is a holistic puzzle**

Wake windows are a big piece, but they're not the *only* piece. If your baby's sleep environment isn't quite right, or feeds are all over the place, you might still struggle even with perfect timing.

This Free Module gives you one critical tool. If you want the complete toolkit for the fourth trimester, my Newborn Guide puts all the pieces together.

Or you're wondering if the swaddle is even safe, and you've gotten yourself into a bit of a spiral reading conflicting information.

I get it. Swaddling can feel like the one thing that's actually *working*, and the idea of taking it away feels like ripping away your only sleep lifeline.

Here's what I want you to know.

The swaddle is a beautiful tool in the newborn period. It mimics the snug feeling of the womb, it dampens the startle reflex, and for many babies it's the difference between a 20-minute nap and a 2-hour one.

But here's the truth: your baby *will* transition out of it. All babies do. And when you do it thoughtfully, when you understand *why* we swaddle and *how* to transition, it doesn't have to tank their sleep.

This module is going to walk you through everything. The safety guidelines, how to know when it's time, and the step-by-step process for transitioning, whether you go cold turkey or gradual.

Now, the swaddle is one piece of your baby's sleep environment. We're also going to touch on safe sleep basics. If you want the full picture for confident, supported newborn sleep, my complete Newborn Guide has everything you need.

But for now, let's tackle this swaddle transition together.

---

### Lesson Text Body

**Welcome to Your Free Module: The Swaddle & Safe Sleep**

Hi, I'm Sally, and I'm so glad you're here.

The swaddle can feel like magic. Finally, something that helps your baby sleep. But I know the questions that come with it:

- Is this safe? How do I know I'm doing it right?
- My baby is starting to roll. When do I stop?
- If I take away the swaddle, will their sleep completely fall apart?

This module is here to give you clarity and confidence.

**What you'll learn:**

- Why we swaddle and what it actually does for your baby
- Current safe sleep guidelines from Red Nose
- How to know when it's time to transition
- Two methods for transitioning: cold turkey vs. gradual
- What to expect (honestly) when those arms come out

**The swaddle isn't forever**

Your baby's startle reflex (the Moro reflex) is strongest in the first 3-4 months. This is why swaddling is so effective. But as your baby develops, that reflex fades, and their need for the swaddle reduces too.

The key is to transition *before* they're actively rolling. A rolling baby with their arms contained is not safe.

**Safety first, always**

I follow Red Nose guidelines for safe sleep. In this module, we'll cover the essentials:
- Back to sleep, every time
- Clear cot, safe bedding
- Arms-out once rolling begins

**Sleep is a holistic puzzle**

The swaddle transition is one moment in your baby's sleep journey. But it's not the only thing that matters. Environment, feeds, settling techniques, and wake windows all play a role.

This Free Module gives you confidence for this one piece. If you want the whole picture, my Newborn Guide has everything for the fourth trimester.

Ready to feel confident about the swaddle transition? Press play on the next lesson.
